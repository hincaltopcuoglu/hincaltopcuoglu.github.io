Understanding Poisson Distribution¶

The Poisson distribution is a discrete probability distribution that expresses the probability of a given number of events occurring in a fixed interval of time or space if these events occur with a known constant mean rate and independently of the time since the last event. It has one parameter: λ (lambda), which is the average number of events per interval.

Poisson Regression: Making Sense of Count Data

Poisson Regression is a specialized regression analysis technique used when the dependent variable is count data. This type of data represents the number of times an event occurs within a specific time interval or space, and it consists of non-negative integers (0, 1, 2, 3,...).

Why Is It Different from Linear Regression? The first question that might come to mind is, "Why not just use a standard Linear Regression for this type of data?" Here are the fundamental reasons:

Distribution Assumption: Linear regression assumes that the errors (residuals) are normally distributed. However, count data often follows a Poisson distribution or similar skewed distributions. Using linear regression for discrete, Poisson-distributed data can lead to inefficient, inconsistent, and contradictory results.

Negative Predictions: A linear regression model can produce negative predictions. However, the count of an event cannot be negative (e.g., -2 accidents). Poisson regression uses a logarithmic link function to ensure that the predictions are always positive.

Constant Variance: Linear regression assumes that the variance is constant (homoscedasticity). In a Poisson distribution, however, the variance is equal to the mean. This means that as the average number of events increases, the variance also increases, which contradicts the assumption of linear regression.

When to Use Poisson Regression?

Poisson regression is ideal for modeling scenarios such as:

Medicine/Biostatistics: The number of seizures a patient experiences in a month while on a specific treatment.

Marketing: Predicting the number of times a customer will purchase a particular product in a year.

Transportation: Modeling the number of accidents at a specific intersection based on factors like traffic volume and weather conditions.

Finance: Estimating the number of fraudulent cases a bank encounters on a monthly basis.

Agriculture: Analyzing the factors that affect the number of pests on a plant.

Key Assumptions:

For a Poisson regression model to yield accurate results, several key assumptions must be met:

Distribution of the Dependent Variable: The dependent variable (the count data we are trying to predict) must follow a Poisson distribution.

Equality of Mean and Variance: The conditional mean of the data must be equal to its conditional variance. This is a fundamental property of the Poisson distribution.

Independence: The observations must be independent of each other.

Log-Linearity: It is assumed that there is a linear relationship between the independent variables and the logarithm of the dependent variable.

Interpreting the Model

In linear regression, the coefficients represent the additive effect on the dependent variable for a one-unit change in the independent variable. In Poisson regression, the coefficients represent the effect on the logarithm of the dependent variable for a one-unit change in the independent variable.

In practice, this means we interpret the coefficients by taking their exponential (e^β). This value represents the multiplicative factor by which the expected count changes when the independent variable increases by one unit.

The Problem of Overdispersion

In real-world applications, the variance of count data is often larger than its mean. This situation is called overdispersion. Overdispersion violates the assumptions of the Poisson regression model and can lead to incorrect conclusions. In such cases, more flexible models that are extensions of the Poisson model, such as Negative Binomial Regression, are preferred.

In summary, when we identify that our dependent variable is count data and observe that its mean and variance are close to each other, using Poisson regression instead of standard linear regression allows our model to make more accurate predictions and is more appropriate for the nature of the data. This directly improves model performance.

So How to find proper poisson function for a variable ? how one can prove that "hey this variable has a significant poisson distribution" ?

This is a critical step in the modeling process. You can't just assume a variable follows a certain distribution; you need to gather evidence to support that assumption.

Here is a step-by-step guide on how to determine the specific Poisson function for your variable and then test if that variable truly follows a Poisson distribution.

Step 1: Finding the "Proper Poisson Function" (Estimating λ)

A Poisson distribution is defined by a single parameter: lambda (λ), which represents the mean (and variance) of the distribution. Therefore, "finding the proper Poisson function" simply means estimating the value of λ from your data.

The best and most common way to estimate λ is to calculate the sample mean of your variable. The sample mean is the Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLE) for the Poisson parameter.

Formula: λ_estimated = (Σ xᵢ) / n = sample mean of the data

So, if you have a variable representing the number of customer complaints per day, you would simply calculate the average number of complaints per day from your historical data. That average becomes your estimated λ.

Step 2: Proving That the Variable Follows a Poisson Distribution

Once you have your estimated λ, you can test the hypothesis: "Does my data follow a Poisson distribution with this λ?" This is not a single check but a combination of methods.

Here are the most common techniques, from simple heuristics to formal statistical tests:

  1. Compare the Mean and Variance

This is the quickest and most important heuristic check.

The core property of a Poisson distribution is that its mean is equal to its variance (μ = σ²).

Action: Calculate the sample mean and the sample variance of your data.

Interpretation:

If variance ≈ mean, you have strong evidence that the data might be Poisson distributed.

If variance > mean, this is a condition known as overdispersion. Your data is more spread out than a Poisson distribution would predict. In this case, a Negative Binomial distribution is often a better fit.

If variance < mean, this is underdispersion, which is less common but can happen.

  1. Visual Check: The Histogram

A powerful method is to plot the frequency distribution of your data and compare it to the theoretical Poisson distribution you've estimated.

Action:

Create a histogram or a bar chart showing the frequency of each count in your data (i.e., how many times you observed 0, 1, 2, 3, etc.).

Using your estimated λ, calculate the theoretical probabilities for each count using the Poisson Probability Mass Function (PMF).

Overlay the theoretical distribution on your data's histogram.

Interpretation: If the shape of your data's histogram closely matches the shape of the theoretical Poisson PMF, it's a very good sign.

  1. Formal Test: The Chi-Squared Goodness-of-Fit Test

This is a formal statistical test that provides a quantitative measure of how well your data fits the Poisson distribution.

Concept: The test compares the observed frequencies of each count in your data with the expected frequencies that a Poisson distribution (with your estimated λ) would predict.

Hypotheses:

Null Hypothesis (H₀): The data follows a Poisson distribution.

Alternative Hypothesis (H₁): The data does not follow a Poisson distribution.

Interpretation (using the p-value):

If p-value > 0.05 (or your chosen significance level, α), you do not reject the null hypothesis. This means there is no significant evidence to say your data is not from a Poisson distribution. This supports your assumption.

If p-value < 0.05, you reject the null hypothesis. This means there is a statistically significant difference between your data and the theoretical Poisson distribution.

But here is the problem might be mean and variance values never equal because of decimal points ? so what is the acceptance rate between them to accept that the hypothesis that they are equal ?

So, you are right to ask for an "acceptance rate" or a practical threshold. While there is no single, universally agreed-upon magic number, here are the standard methods and rules of thumb used by statisticians and data scientists to decide if the mean and variance are "close enough."

  1. The Dispersion Index (The Variance-to-Mean Ratio): The most common and useful metric is the Dispersion Index (also called the dispersion parameter). Dispersion Index = Sample Variance / Sample Mean For a perfect Poisson distribution, the Dispersion Index is exactly 1.0. Dispersion Index > 1.0 indicates Overdispersion. The variance is greater than the mean. Dispersion Index < 1.0 indicates Underdispersion. The variance is less than the mean.

Rules of Thumb for the Dispersion Index: There isn't a strict cutoff, but here is a widely used guideline: 0.8 to 1.2: Generally considered "close enough" to 1.0. If your index falls in this range, you can often proceed with a Poisson model with reasonable confidence. 1.2 to 1.5 (or 0.5 to 0.8): This is a gray area. It suggests some deviation from the Poisson assumption. You should be cautious and might want to compare the performance of a Poisson model with an alternative, like a Negative Binomial model.

1.5 (or < 0.5): This is strong evidence that the Poisson assumption is violated. A standard Poisson model will likely produce incorrect standard errors and p-values, leading to flawed conclusions. In the case of overdispersion (>1.5), you should strongly consider using a Negative Binomial Regression model instead.

Important Context: The larger your sample size, the more seriously you should take smaller deviations from 1.0. A dispersion index of 1.1 in a massive dataset might be statistically significant, whereas in a small dataset of 30 points, it's likely just noise.

  1. Statistical Tests for Dispersion For a more formal approach, you can perform a statistical test where the null hypothesis is that the mean and variance are equal. A common method is the likelihood-ratio test, which compares the Poisson model (which fixes dispersion at 1) to a more flexible model like the Negative Binomial model (which estimates dispersion as a parameter). If the test shows a statistically significant result (e.g., p-value < 0.05), it provides evidence that the extra dispersion parameter in the Negative Binomial model is necessary, meaning your data is not truly Poisson.

def analyze_dispersion(data, name):
    """Calculates and interprets the dispersion of a dataset."""
    mean = np.mean(data)
    variance = np.var(data, ddof=1) # Use ddof=1 for the sample variance
    dispersion_index = variance / mean

    print(f"--- Analysis for: {name} ---")
    print(f"Sample Mean:    {mean:.3f}")
    print(f"Sample Variance: {variance:.3f}")
    print(f"Dispersion Index (Var/Mean): {dispersion_index:.3f}")

    if 0.8 <= dispersion_index <= 1.2:
        print("Conclusion: Looks consistent with a Poisson distribution.\n")
    elif dispersion_index > 1.2:
        print("Conclusion: Evidence of OVERDISPERSION. A Poisson model may not be appropriate.\n")
    else: # dispersion_index < 0.8
        print("Conclusion: Evidence of UNDERDISPERSION. A Poisson model may not be appropriate.\n")

So if we prove that a variable has poisson distribution but target variable not, what we should do ?

The distribution of your target variable (Y) dictates your choice of model. The distribution of your independent variables/features (X) is far less important and often doesn't violate model assumptions.

Let's break this down. In scenarioS: An Independent Variable (X) has a proven Poisson distribution (e.g., "number of support calls a customer made last month"). The Target Variable (Y) is something else entirely (e.g., "total amount of money the customer spent," which is continuous, or "whether the customer churned," which is binary).

Here is what you should do, depending on the nature of your target variable.

The Guiding Principle: Match the Model to the Target (Y) The core assumptions of statistical models are almost always about the distribution of the target variable or the model's errors (residuals), not the features.

Scenario 1: Your Target Variable (Y) is Continuous (e.g., revenue, temperature, housing price) If your target is a continuous value, you should be thinking about models like Linear Regression, Ridge, Lasso, or more complex models like Gradient Boosting for regression. Model Choice: A regression model (e.g., LinearRegression, XGBRegressor). What to do with your Poisson feature (X)? Use it directly! Explanation: Linear Regression's main assumption is that the residuals (the prediction errors) are normally distributed, not that the target or features are. There is no assumption in linear regression that independent variables must be normally distributed. You can have skewed, binary, or Poisson-distributed features. The model will simply learn a coefficient for your Poisson feature, representing the linear relationship between the number of events (your X) and the continuous outcome (your Y).

Scenario 2: Your Target Variable (Y) is Binary/Categorical (e.g., churn vs. no-churn, fraud vs. not-fraud, cat vs. dog) If your target is a category, you should be using a classification model. Model Choice: A classification model (e.g., LogisticRegression, RandomForestClassifier, SVC). What to do with your Poisson feature (X)? Use it directly! Explanation: Logistic Regression: Assumes a linear relationship between the features and the log-odds of the outcome. The distribution of the feature itself doesn't matter. Tree-Based Models (Random Forest, XGBoost): These models are non-parametric and make no assumptions about the distribution of your features. They work by finding optimal split points. A Poisson-distributed feature is perfectly fine for them. In fact, tree-based models are often the best choice when you have features with unusual distributions.

Scenario 3: Your Target Variable (Y) is also a Count, but you proved it's NOT Poisson (e.g., number of items purchased, but the variance is much larger than the mean) This is the most interesting case. You have a count target, but you've already done your due diligence and found that a Poisson model is inappropriate. Model Choice: Choose a model that fits the actual distribution of your target variable. If Y is Overdispersed (Variance > Mean): This is the most common scenario for count data. The correct model is Negative Binomial Regression. It is a generalization of Poisson regression that includes an extra parameter to handle the higher variance. If Y has an Excess of Zeros: If your data has far more zeros than a Poisson or Negative Binomial model would predict (e.g., most customers don't buy an extended warranty, but a few buy one), you should use Zero-Inflated Models (Zero-Inflated Poisson or Zero-Inflated Negative Binomial). What to do with your Poisson feature (X)? Use it directly! Explanation: The choice of Negative Binomial or Zero-Inflated regression is entirely driven by the behavior of your target variable (Y). The fact that one of your features (X) happens to be well-behaved and follows a Poisson distribution is great, but it doesn't change the model you need for your target.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.api as sm
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import seaborn as sns

# --- Data Generation with a Two-Stage Process ---
np.random.seed(42)
n_samples = 500

# Features remain the same
temperature = np.random.normal(loc=18, scale=7, size=n_samples)
is_weekend = np.random.choice([0, 1], size=n_samples, p=[0.7, 0.3])

# True coefficients for the average rate remain the same
intercept_true = 1.0
beta_temp_true = 0.07
beta_weekend_true = 0.6

# Calculate the EXPECTED MEAN (mu) based on our features
# This is the average around which our lambda will vary
mu_values = np.exp(intercept_true + (beta_temp_true * temperature) + (beta_weekend_true * is_weekend))


# --- Stage 1: Introduce Randomness to the Rate (Lambda) ---
# We'll use a Gamma distribution to create our random lambdas.
# A key property is that we can set the Gamma distribution's mean to be our mu.
# The 'shape' parameter controls the variance. A smaller shape parameter means more variance.
shape_param = 50  # Let's call this our "heterogeneity factor". Lower value = more dispersion.
scale_param = mu_values / shape_param

# Now, for each data point, draw a unique lambda from the Gamma distribution
lambda_values_randomized = np.random.gamma(shape=shape_param, scale=scale_param, size=n_samples)


# --- Stage 2: Generate the Final Count from the Randomized Lambda ---
# Use the unique, noisy lambda for each data point to generate the final count
num_rentals_overdispersed = np.random.poisson(lambda_values_randomized)


# --- Create the DataFrame ---
df_robust = pd.DataFrame({
    'num_rentals': num_rentals_overdispersed,
    'temperature': temperature,
    'is_weekend': is_weekend,
    'expected_mu': mu_values, # The "clean" average rate
    'random_lambda': lambda_values_randomized # The "noisy" rate used for generation
})

print("--- Sample of the Robustly Generated Data ---")

Let's break down exactly what this summary is telling us and, more importantly, what it's hiding. Analysis of the Poisson Model Results This is a classic example of a model that looks great on the surface but is fundamentally flawed because it's based on a wrong assumption.

  1. The Good News (The Part That Isn't Wrong) Coefficients (coef): Intercept: 0.9867 temperature: 0.0707 is_weekend: 0.5932 Look how close these are to the "true" parameters we used to generate the data (Intercept=1.0, temperature=0.07, is_weekend=0.6). This is a key insight: even with overdispersion, a Poisson model often does a decent job of estimating the average relationship. It correctly identified that higher temperature and weekends increase rentals.

  2. The Bad News (The Deceptive Part) The problem lies entirely in the model's assessment of its own certainty. Standard Errors (std err): temperature: 0.002 is_weekend: 0.025 These numbers are extremely small. The model is essentially saying, "I am incredibly certain about the effect of temperature and weekends." Why? Because it assumes the variance is much lower than it actually is. It's blind to the 694% extra variance (dispersion rate of 6.94) and is therefore completely underestimating the real-world noise and uncertainty.

Z-scores (z) and P-values (P>|z|): The z-score is calculated as coef / std err. Because the standard errors are artificially tiny, the z-scores are enormous (42.449 for temperature!). An enormous z-score mathematically leads to a p-value that is effectively zero (0.000).

The Grand Conclusion: A Story of Overconfidence This model summary tells a story of false precision and overconfidence. An analyst who didn't check for overdispersion would look at this and report: "The effect of temperature on bike rentals is positive and statistically significant with a p-value of essentially zero. We are virtually certain about this relationship." But we know better. We know the data has massive variability that this model is ignoring. The true conclusion should be: "Temperature appears to have a positive effect, but there is a very high degree of unexplained variability in the data, and our confidence in the precise effect size is much lower than this model suggests." The Poisson model has led us to a conclusion that is dangerously misleading about the certainty of our findings.

Why it fits: Count Data: The target variable is the number of goals (0, 1, 2, 3...), a non-negative integer. Relatively Rare: Compared to the number of passes or tackles, goals are rare events within a 90-minute match. Largely Independent: One goal being scored doesn't necessarily make another goal more or less likely in the next minute (this is debatable, but it's a reasonable starting assumption). Stable Rate: For a given team playing against a specific opponent, we can think of them as having an average goal-scoring "rate" or potential. Project Workflow: Proving Poisson is the Right Choice The Scenario: We will predict the number of goals a team scores in a match based on simple features like is_home_team and the opponent they are playing. Get the Data: We'll use a publicly available dataset of English Premier League match results. EDA (The Moment of Truth): We will calculate the dispersion index for the number of goals scored. Our hypothesis is that it will be very close to 1.0. Modeling: We will fit a Poisson Regression model. The important notes: The goal is not to show that statistical distributions are useless, but to show their proper place in the modern data science workflow. The statistical analysis we did was not a "useless" step; it was the essential foundation upon which our final, most powerful model was built and validated.

The Wrong Takeaway vs. The Correct Takeaway The Wrong Takeaway: "Statistics is old, ML is new. Always skip to XGBoost for the best results." The Correct Takeaway: "Statistical understanding makes you a more effective and responsible machine learning practitioner. It provides the essential tools for sanity-checking your data, building intelligent baselines, and interpreting your results."

Here are the specific, undeniable reasons why the Poisson/Negative Binomial analysis was critical and not useless:

  1. It Provided the Intelligent Baseline How do we know our XGBoost RMSE of 1.0318 is "good"? It's just a number in a vacuum. We only know it's good because we compared it to a strong, theory-driven baseline: the Negative Binomial model. The NB model, chosen because our EDA told us the data was overdispersed counts, gave us an RMSE of 1.1463. This is our benchmark. Now we can say, "Our XGBoost model provides a 10% accuracy improvement over a well-chosen statistical model." Without that benchmark, we have no context for our results.

  2. It Forced Us to Understand Our Data (EDA) The entire process of checking for a Poisson distribution (calculating mean, variance, dispersion index) forced us to deeply understand the nature of our target variable. This is the single most important step in any modeling project. A student who just imports xgboost and runs .fit() has no idea if their data is skewed, has outliers, or follows any logical pattern. They are flying blind. Our statistical approach ensured we knew exactly what kind of data we were dealing with.

  3. It Answers the "Why" (Inference) This is the most significant difference. XGBoost tells us what will happen. It's a predictive powerhouse. The Negative Binomial model tells us why it happens. It gives us a simple table of coefficients. We can say with confidence, "Aston Villa, when playing away, tends to score exp(-0.4318) times the rate of the baseline team, holding all else constant." This is inference. It's the ability to explain the relationships in your data. For countless business and scientific problems, this is far more valuable than a black-box prediction.

  4. It Helped Us Build a Better XGBoost Model Our statistical knowledge directly improved our XGBoost model. Because we knew the target variable was count data that followed a Poisson-like process, we specifically chose the objective='count:poisson' for our XGBoost regressor. A student without this knowledge would have used the default objective='reg:squarederror', which is optimized for normally distributed errors. Our theory-driven choice of objective function almost certainly led to a more accurate and better-calibrated XGBoost model.

The Final Analogy for Students: The Doctor's Diagnosis Think of it like a doctor treating a patient. The EDA and Statistical Modeling (checking mean/variance, fitting a Negative Binomial model) is like the initial diagnosis. The doctor uses a stethoscope, checks temperature, asks questions, and uses their fundamental knowledge of biology. They try to understand the nature of the illness. The XGBoost Model is the powerful, advanced treatment.

You would never want a doctor who skips the diagnosis and just gives every patient the strongest medicine they have. They must first understand the problem. Our statistical analysis was the diagnosis. It gave us the insight and the benchmark needed to apply the powerful treatment (XGBoost) effectively and responsibly. The final lesson: Don't be the data scientist who just knows how to use the powerful medicine. Be the one who also knows how to diagnose the patient. That is the path to becoming a truly valuable and effective practitioner.

import statsmodels.formula.api as smf
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

from scipy import stats

# --- Step 1: Creating the Synthetic Dataset ---
print("--- Step 1: Creating Synthetic Dataset... ---")
np.random.seed(42)
n_samples = 1000

# Predictor 1: Poisson Distribution
x1_poisson = np.random.poisson(lam=5, size=n_samples)

# Predictor 2: Normal Distribution
x2_normal = np.random.normal(loc=10, scale=2, size=n_samples)

# Predictor 3: Indeterminate/Skewed Distribution (Log-Normal)
x3_skewed = np.random.lognormal(mean=2, sigma=0.5, size=n_samples)

# Let's define the true coefficients (the hidden values the model will try to learn)
beta_0 = 0.5  # Intercept
beta_1 = 0.05 # Effect of the Poisson predictor
beta_2 = 0.1  # Effect of the Normal predictor
beta_3 = 0.01 # Effect of the Skewed predictor

# Calculate the expected value (lambda) of the target variable
# log(lambda) = beta_0 + beta_1*x1 + beta_2*x2 + beta_3*x3
log_lambda = beta_0 + (beta_1 * x1_poisson) + (beta_2 * x2_normal) + (beta_3 * x3_skewed)
lambda_values = np.exp(log_lambda)

# TARGET VARIABLE: Create the target variable from a Poisson distribution
y_poisson = np.random.poisson(lam=lambda_values, size=n_samples)

# Let's collect the data in a DataFrame
df = pd.DataFrame({
    'y_poisson': y_poisson,
    'x1_poisson': x1_poisson,
    'x2_normal': x2_normal,
    'x3_skewed': x3_skewed
})

print("Dataset created. First 5 rows:")
print(df.head())
print("\nDistribution of the target variable (should be Poisson):")
print(f"Mean: {df['y_poisson'].mean():.2f}, Variance: {df['y_poisson'].var():.2f}")


# --- Step 2: Training the Poisson Regression Model ---
print("\n--- Step 2: Training Poisson Regression Model... ---")
formula = "y_poisson ~ x1_poisson + x2_normal + x3_skewed"
poisson_model = smf.glm(formula=formula, data=df, family=sm.families.Poisson()).fit()

# Calculate Deviance Residuals (the most appropriate type of residual for GLMs)

Detailed Analysis¶

There are two major and unexpected results in this experiment:

  1. Prediction Performance: The "simpler" statistical model (Poisson GLM) beat the "more complex" machine learning model (XGBoost).
  2. Feature Importance: XGBoost incorrectly identified the true order of importance of the variables.

Let's examine both of these incredible results separately.

1. Prediction Performance: Why Did the "Simpler" Model Win?¶

Metric Poisson GLM (Winner) XGBoost
RMSE 2.6064 2.7406
MAE 2.0824 2.1875

This result contains a very important lesson that shatters the misconception that "the most complex model is always the best." The reason the Poisson GLM won is related to the nature of this problem:

  • "Home Field Advantage": We created this dataset using the exact mathematical formula (λ = exp(β₀ + β₁*x₁ + ...) ) that the Poisson GLM is designed to solve. In other words, we asked the Poisson GLM a question for which it "knew the right answer." The model perfectly captured the simple, smooth, and log-linear relationship underlying the data.

  • XGBoost's Approach: XGBoost does not know this underlying formula. It tries to approximate this relationship by creating thousands of simple "if-then" rules (decision trees). For example, it takes thousands of small steps like, "if x2 > 10.5, then the prediction is this, otherwise it's that." This approach is incredibly powerful at capturing complex and non-linear patterns. However, when it tries to "imitate" a smooth and simple pattern like the one in our data, it leaves small imperfections.

Analogy: This is like drawing a circle. The Poisson GLM had a compass (the correct formula) and drew a perfect circle. XGBoost had a ruler and tried to draw the circle with thousands of tiny, straight lines. The result looked very much like a circle, but it couldn't be as flawless as the one drawn with the compass.

2. Feature Importance: Why Did XGBoost Get It Wrong?¶

  • True Order of Importance (According to the betas we defined):

    1. x2_normal (β₂=0.1)
    2. x1_poisson (β₁=0.05)
    3. x3_skewed (β₃=0.01)

  • Order of Importance Found by XGBoost:

    1. x3_skewed
    2. x2_normal
    3. x1_poisson

The reason XGBoost got this order wrong is because it defines the word "importance" differently:

  • "Importance" for Poisson GLM: It is the magnitude of a coefficient (coef). This directly measures the size of the effect (effect size) of that variable on the target.
  • "Importance" for XGBoost (by default): It is the number of times a variable is used to split a branch (frequency/weight) across all the trees in the model. This measures the utility of that feature in the model's prediction-making process.

The x3_skewed variable, being right-skewed and having a wide range, may have offered many "useful" points for splitting the branches of the trees. This could have led to it being used frequently by the model, despite its small overall effect. This situation is a great proof that XGBoost's feature importance does not always mean the same thing as a variable's true effect size.

  1.   2. Statistical Models Answer the "Why" Question: The Poisson GLM not only gave us a prediction but also clearly told us the effect of each variable (coef) and the reality of that effect (p-value). This is invaluable for understanding.

  3. Machine Learning Models Answer the "What Will Happen" Question: XGBoost's purpose is not to understand, but to make the best prediction. Although it couldn't make a better prediction in this experiment, it will outperform statistical models in the majority of more complex, real-world problems.
